The In-Tray

You are the desk officer. Files arrive. You decide.

About fifteen files, five minutes, no explanation given in advance.

Each file has one paragraph of context and a small, non-disqualifying defect. You have three options: Approve, Refuse, or Request Further Documents. Your score reflects your career, not how many files you clear. Nothing else will be explained in advance; the point is to find out how the incentives work by working the desk.

Every dossier here is synthetic, invented for this game and not drawn from any real case file. The payoff structure it teaches is the argument, detailed after you play in the debrief.

The hidden payoff structure

Nothing above was random noise: every session runs on the same fixed, hidden payoffs. This is what you were actually responding to:

ActionVisible cost nowDelayed costResolves the file?
ApproveNone~16 to 50% chance of an audit flag, scaling with how many times the file was previously deferredYes, permanently
RefuseBacklog count rises, and a small ambient score drag beginsFile returns in 3 to 6 rounds, same defectNo
Request Further DocumentsNone visible anywhereFile returns in 2 to 4 rounds, same defectNo

Scroll sideways to see the full table on narrow screens.

Request Further Documents is not a neutral, cautious option: it is the strictly dominant one under this payoff structure, with zero visible cost, no blame exposure, and no backlog attribution. That is the mechanism this game is arguing for. Bureaucratic deferral does not require a will to obstruct, only a payoff structure where every individually rational move defers, and where a file can be "in progress" indefinitely without anyone being at fault for it. It formalizes the logic Christopher Hood describes as blame avoidance and Christopher Anderson's decision-avoidance account of choice deferral; see the reading map for both, and for where this differs from a claim that officials are lazy or malicious.

Epistemic status. This game demonstrates a payoff structure's logic; it does not measure a real institution. The probabilities are designed to make the incentive gradient legible in fifteen rounds, not calibrated to any dataset. Turning this into an empirical claim rather than a demonstration would mean coding real dated document sequences the way the decline simulation's companion piece describes, and testing whether the same asymmetry holds.
