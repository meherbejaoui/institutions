Five ways to explain why nothing happens

The account of trust and institutional decline behind the other two pieces on this site doesn't stand alone: it sits among five established frames for why institutions fail to decide. This page maps each one honestly, including what it explains, what it leaves open, and where the account-of-trust mechanism differs from it.

On these citations. Every reference below was checked against a publisher, journal, or indexing record before publication. The full verification trail, including one page-range discrepancy across secondary sources that I flag rather than silently resolve, is in REFERENCES.md.

The core account, stated once

Officials and members hold something like an account of trust and hope in their institution. As that account drains under visible decline, the people with the best outside options exit first, and each departure removes exactly the capacity the institution needed to correct itself, accelerating the decline for whoever remains. The claim is that this makes decision avoidance a property of declining systems, not simply a series of individual failures: a pattern that becomes visible in the interactive pieces on this site, and one that would need interview evidence on officials' actual trust levels, which no administrative record can substitute for, to actually test.

1. Non-decision-making: Bachrach & Baratz

Peter Bachrach and Morton S. Baratz, "Two Faces of Power," American Political Science Review 56, no. 4 (1962): 947–952; "Decisions and Nondecisions: An Analytical Framework," American Political Science Review 57, no. 3 (1963): 632–642.

Power isn't only exercised by winning a decision: it's exercised by keeping an issue off the agenda entirely, by manipulating "the dominant community values, myths, and political institutions and procedures" so that only safe questions ever reach a formal decision. A non-decision is itself a decision, made invisible.

Explains
Why some administrative defects never even reach a moment of formal choice, filtered out before the desk sees them.
Leaves open
Why an issue that does reach the desk, like every file in the In-Tray game, still doesn't get resolved once it's already on the agenda.

2. Blame avoidance: Hood; Bach & Wegrich

Christopher Hood, The Blame Game: Spin, Bureaucracy, and Self-Preservation in Government (Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press, 2011). Tobias Bach and Kai Wegrich, eds., The Blind Spots of Public Bureaucracy and the Politics of Non-Coordination (Cham: Palgrave Macmillan, 2018), esp. their introductory chapter, "Blind Spots, Biased Attention, and the Politics of Non-Coordination."

Officials and organizations act to minimize the risk of being blamed for a bad outcome, often at the direct expense of good policy. Hood catalogs the tactics: presentational spin, agency strategies, and policy strategies designed to diffuse or delay responsibility. Bach and Wegrich extend this into coordination failures: bureaucracies develop structural "blind spots" that let responsibility diffuse across boundaries nobody is individually accountable for crossing.

Explains
The In-Tray game's core asymmetry directly: Approve carries visible blame risk, Request Documents carries none. That gradient is blame avoidance, formalized as a payoff structure.
Leaves open
Blame avoidance is a strategy individual officials or agencies choose. It doesn't by itself explain why the same defect can recur for years across different officials and different reissues, which is closer to a property of the system than a choice any one actor keeps making.

3. Implementation deficit: Pressman & Wildavsky

Jeffrey L. Pressman and Aaron Wildavsky, Implementation: How Great Expectations in Washington Are Dashed in Oakland (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1973).

Policies fail not because anyone opposes them, but because implementation requires a long chain of separate actors to each say yes, and the probability of complete agreement collapses multiplicatively with every additional veto point. Pressman and Wildavsky's famous case counted dozens of clearance points for a single Oakland jobs program. No villain is required; the structure alone produces failure.

Explains
Why a file with many clearance points is structurally more likely to stall somewhere, independent of anyone's incentives at any single point.
Leaves open
The account-of-trust mechanism is about a single official's repeated deferral of the same file over years, which isn't a multi-actor coordination problem: it's one desk, one defect, one choice made and remade.

4. Individual decision avoidance: Anderson

Christopher J. Anderson, "The Psychology of Doing Nothing: Forms of Decision Avoidance Result from Reason and Emotion," Psychological Bulletin 129, no. 1 (2003): 139–167 (cited elsewhere as 139–166; see REFERENCES.md).

A psychological synthesis across choice deferral, status-quo bias, omission bias, and inaction inertia: people avoid deciding when a choice is difficult, when it risks regret, or when inaction is easier to justify after the fact than action is. Deferral is a coping response to a hard trade-off, not a failure of will.

Explains
The psychological pull toward Request Documents over Approve in the In-Tray game: it's the option that lets the officer avoid committing to a judgment on genuinely ambiguous facts.
Leaves open
Anderson's account is about an individual chooser facing one decision. It says little about what happens when many individually rational avoidances aggregate into an institution-wide pattern that outlasts any one person's tenure at the desk.

5. Exit, voice, and loyalty: Hirschman

Albert O. Hirschman, Exit, Voice, and Loyalty: Responses to Decline in Firms, Organizations, and States (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1970).

When an organization declines, its members have two possible responses: exit (leave for an alternative) or voice (complain and try to fix it from within), with loyalty determining how much decline someone tolerates before choosing either. Hirschman's key insight is that the two responses trade off: easy exit can starve an organization of the voice it needed to hear in order to recover, especially from its most capable members, who usually have the best outside options.

Explains
The entire mechanism the decline simulation dramatizes: capable people leaving first, and that departure directly undermining the institution's remaining capacity to self-correct.
Leaves open
Hirschman's original framework is pitched at firms, organizations, and states broadly, using market-style exit as the paradigm case. It says less about administrative career structures with limited exit options, or about what happens to voice specifically when it is heard but structurally ignored: the responsiveness parameter the simulation lets you tune.

Where the account-of-trust mechanism sits

None of the five frames above is wrong; each explains a real piece of why institutions fail to decide. The account-of-trust mechanism doesn't replace them: it proposes a specific link between two of them that neither states directly, namely that Hirschman's exit and voice dynamics are the mechanism underneath Hood's blame avoidance and Anderson's decision avoidance in a declining system, because each exit of a capable, high-standing member doesn't just reduce voice, it removes exactly the capacity that would have made blame avoidance and decision avoidance more costly to indulge in. That is a specific, falsifiable claim about a causal channel, and it is the one that would need interview data, not administrative records alone, to actually test.
