Research demonstrations

Why capable people leave, or fall silent

Three small, playable pieces built around one question: inside an institution that is visibly declining, why does decision-making stall, and can the spiral of exit and silence be interrupted before it removes the capacity needed to fix it?

That question comes out of roughly eight years in Tunisian public administration, five of them overseeing state-owned enterprises and public establishments from the Presidency of the Government. These three pieces exist because a research question is easier to argue than to show, so this tries to show it: one simulation you can run, one you can play, and one map of where the idea sits relative to the literature it's in conversation with.

On accuracy. Every citation on this site is checked against a verifiable source before publication; see REFERENCES.md for the audit trail. The simulation and the game are illustrative models built to make a mechanism legible, not empirical findings. Neither claims to prove a theory; where a piece runs on synthetic data rather than real institutional records, it says so on the page.

The three pieces

Simulation

The Decline Spiral

An agent-based model of an institution and the people inside it. Each person holds a depletable account of trust; as it drains, the ones with the best outside options leave first, and every departure removes exactly the capacity the institution needed to correct course. Adjust the sliders (trust decay, whether voice is actually heard, how many people have somewhere else to go) and watch for the point where a recoverable institution tips into a spiral it can't pull out of.

The In-Tray

You are the official. Dossiers arrive; you can approve, refuse, or request further documents. The game never tells you the payoffs; you discover them by playing, the same way an incentive structure teaches itself to anyone who sits at that desk long enough. At the end, the choices you made are turned back into the evidence a researcher would collect: a deferral-chain timeline and a survival curve, built entirely from your own session.

What this is for

These are built to be useful on their own terms first: the simulation and the game work as teaching tools for anyone covering Hirschman or bureaucratic delay, independent of anything about me. They're also, more simply, the clearest way I know to show what this research question looks like in practice. Corrections and feedback are welcome via GitHub.
